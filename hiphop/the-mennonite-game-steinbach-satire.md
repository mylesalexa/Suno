# The Mennonite Game *(comedic folk-polka — Steinbach, Manitoba satire, amped up 100x)*

*One-line concept: population of Steinbach, Manitoba — everybody's cousin. This song is the actual "Mennonite Game" (the real genealogy-guessing ritual Mennonites play when they meet) turned into an accordion-driven novelty anthem, with the town's origin story, its food, and its passive-aggressive politeness all played for maximum affectionate satire.*

*In-group cultural satire, not mockery — modeled directly on "The Daily Bonnet" (a real satirical Mennonite news site out of Steinbach) and the long, well-documented tradition of Mennonites telling jokes about themselves. Every bit here is a real, sourced cultural detail turned up to 100x, not an invented stereotype.*

---

## Research — What's Actually True Here

- **Steinbach was founded in 1874 by eighteen Plautdietsch-speaking Mennonite families** who'd emigrated from the Borosenko colony (in present-day Ukraine). Founding surnames included Wiebe, Penner, Reimer, Toews, Friesen, and Plett — every surname in the chorus is a real founding-family name. ([Manitoba Historical Society](http://www.mhs.mb.ca/docs/sites/steinbachautocity.shtml) · [Wikipedia: Steinbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinbach,_Manitoba))
- **The "Automobile City" nickname has a genuinely perfect irony behind it:** Jacob Reimer Friesen opened the first Ford dealership in western Canada in Steinbach in 1914 — and was **excommunicated by the church for bringing the first car to town.** By the 1950s, the town had rebranded itself as a car-dealership hub and coined "The Automobile City" as a civic slogan. That's not an exaggeration — that's the actual history. ([Manitoba Historical Society](http://www.mhs.mb.ca/docs/sites/steinbachautocity.shtml))
- **"The Mennonite Game" is a real, widely-documented social ritual**, not an invented bit: two Mennonites meeting for the first time trade last names and rapidly trace their family connections, because generations of marrying within the community means almost everyone with a Mennonite surname is related somehow. There's an entire website (mennonite.games) and published guides ("How to Win at the Mennonite Game") dedicated to it. ([MHSC](https://mhsc.ca/mennos/cmennonit.html) · [The Unger Review](https://www.ungerreview.com/win-mennonite-guide/))
- **"The Daily Bonnet"** (now "The Unger Review"), created by Andrew Unger in 2016, is a real satirical Mennonite news site — the actual model for this song's tone. Real published headlines include "Mennonite Church Discards Hymnals Because No One Can Read Music Anymore" and "Mennonite Groundhog Predicts Early Paska Buns This Year." It's been cited in the Manitoba Legislature and referenced in the Canadian House of Commons as an example of Mennonite humor. The bridge's fake headline is a direct homage to this exact style. ([Wikipedia: The Daily Bonnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Bonnet))
- **Faspa** (a light Sunday-afternoon coffee meal), **schmaunt fat** (a sweet cream gravy poured on nearly everything), and **borscht** are all real, specific Mennonite food traditions — the running joke in the actual community is about eating too much schmaunt fat and too little at faspa itself. Thrift-store culture (MCC thrift stores specifically) is also a genuinely well-worn joke inside the community, not an outsider's invention. ([Anabaptist World](https://anabaptistworld.org/a-funny-thing-happened-on-the-way-to-the-thrift-store/))

**Why this matters for how the song was written:** every punchline here is a real cultural detail turned up, not a guess at what might be funny. That's the difference between satire that lands as an inside joke and satire that reads as an outside mock — and it's the exact quality that makes Mennonite comedy (per the "That Mennonite Joke" documentary) work as well as it does across a huge range of listeners, including people with zero connection to the culture.

## Concept & Angle

**Concept:** The song IS the Mennonite Game — the chorus is the actual ritual, turned into a singalong. Verse 1 tells Steinbach's real founding story and lands on the genuinely absurd true irony of "Automobile City" (a man excommunicated for owning a car, in a town now built around car dealerships). Verse 2 stacks up real, specific cultural details — faspa, schmaunt fat, borscht, thrift stores, and the famous Mennonite indirectness ("ja, well, no" as a complete sentence) — into rapid-fire, affectionate exaggeration. The bridge is a direct homage to The Daily Bonnet's actual headline style.

**Four things that make this land as affectionate satire, not mockery:**
1. **Every joke is sourced, not invented** — the excommunication story, the Mennonite Game, the Daily Bonnet headlines are all real. Turning up something true is satire; inventing something false about a group is just mockery.
2. **The targets are behaviors and institutions (the church's early car ban, thrift-store culture, indirect speech patterns), never appearance, ethnicity, or anything that would read as a slur.**
3. **The tone is celebratory, not condescending** — "we're all one big family tree" is the emotional center, and it's warm, not sneering.
4. **The model is in-group comedy that already exists and is beloved** (The Daily Bonnet, "That Mennonite Joke" documentary) — this isn't a genre being invented from outside; it's joining a tradition that's already there.

**Why folk-polka instead of the catalog's usual 80s lanes:** Steinbach's Mennonites trace their heritage through Russia and Ukraine back to Germanic Anabaptist roots — an accordion-driven, oom-pah-bounce polka is the genuinely correct musical DNA for this content, not a retrofit. It's also inherently a comedic sound (polka has been the novelty-song chassis of choice for generations), which matches "amp the comedy up 100x" better than a straight pop or rap treatment would.

## Style / Tempo / Vocal

**Genre:** **comedic folk-polka** — oom-pah accordion as the lead instrument, tuba/bass carrying the bounce, a light fiddle accent, hand claps on the backbeat. Bright, cheerful, novelty-song energy throughout.

- **Tempo:** **126 BPM** — a lively, danceable polka bounce.
- **Key:** **D major** — bright and traditional for the genre.
- **Vocal:** playful, exaggerated **male lead** on the verses — half storyteller, half auctioneer. Full group singalong on the chorus, cheerful and communal.
